WHO AND NCDC INDIA NATIONAL SNAKEBITE EMERGENCY GUIDELINES

[SECTION: GENERAL FIRST AID PROTOCOL]
1. Reassure the victim: Anxiety and excitement increase heart rate and accelerate the spread of venom through the lymphatic system. Reassure the victim that most bites are non-venomous and treatment is readily available.
2. Immobilize the bitten limb: Immobilize the patient completely. Use a rigid splint or makeshift wooden board to keep the bitten arm or leg completely still. Any movement or muscle contraction pumps venom into systemic circulation.
3. Remove tight items: Immediately remove rings, bracelets, watches, anklets, footwear, or tight restrictive clothing from the bitten limb. Swelling occurs rapidly and tight items act as accidental tourniquets, leading to severe ischemia and tissue necrosis.
4. Transport immediately: Rapid transfer to a secondary or tertiary hospital or Primary Health Center (PHC) equipped with Polyvalent Anti-Snake Venom (ASV) and ICU facilities is the single most important factor in saving lives.

[SECTION: STRICT FIRST AID DON'TS]
1. DO NOT apply tight arterial tourniquets, ropes, wire, or shoelaces. Tourniquets cause severe local tissue damage, gangrene, ischemia, and increase the risk of limb amputation. Sudden removal of a tight tourniquet can cause a fatal surge of venom into circulation.
2. DO NOT make cuts, incisions, or scarification at the bite site. Cutting does not remove venom, causes severe blood loss, and introduces serious bacterial infections.
3. DO NOT attempt to suck venom by mouth or mechanical suction devices. Suction is ineffective and introduces oral bacteria to the wound.
4. DO NOT apply herbal pastes, traditional remedies, black stones, ice packs, chemicals, or electric shocks. These delay medical care and worsen tissue damage.
5. DO NOT give alcohol, caffeine, or aspirin/NSAID pain medication. Aspirin increases bleeding risks in haemotoxic envenomations. Paracetamol may be administered for pain.

[SECTION: SYMPTOM RECOGNITION AND TOXICITY TYPES]
1. Neurotoxic Envenoming (Cobras and Kraits): Characterized by early drooping eyelids (Ptosis), facial muscle weakness, difficulty swallowing (Dysphagia), slurred speech, double vision, and progressive respiratory failure. Common Krait bites often occur at night, cause minimal local pain or swelling, and present as early morning abdominal pain and sudden weakness.
2. Haemotoxic Envenoming (Vipers): Characterized by severe local pain, rapid swelling, blistering, persistent oozing of blood from the bite wound, spontaneous bleeding from gums/nose, dark brown/red urine (hematuria/myoglobinuria), and low blood pressure (shock).
3. Position during transport: Place the victim on their left side in the recovery position with their head turned down to keep airway clear in case of vomiting.

[SECTION: MEDICAL ANTIVENOM FACT SHEET]
1. Polyvalent Anti-Snake Venom (ASV) in India: Neutralizes venom of the "Big Four" species—Indian Cobra (Naja naja), Common Krait (Bungarus caeruleus), Russell's Viper (Daboia russelii), and Saw-scaled Viper (Echis carinatus).
2. ASV must only be administered by trained healthcare professionals when clinical signs of envenoming (coagulopathy or neurotoxicity) are confirmed.
3. Treatment should never be delayed waiting for exact species identification.
